What's a Chladni plate?

In 1787 the German physicist and musician Ernst Chladni scattered sand on a thin brass plate and drew a violin bow along its edge. The plate rang out a note, and the sand jumped into a sharp geometric figure.

A vibrating plate doesn't move everywhere at once. Some regions swing up and down hard while certain lines stay perfectly still: the nodal lines. The sand is thrown off the moving parts and piles up on the still lines, so the figure you see is the shape of the sound. Every note has its own figure, and higher notes draw finer ones.

Why it's worth a look

  • It makes sound visible. Each pitch leaves a pattern you can see, not only hear.
  • It moved science forward. Napoleon saw Chladni's demonstration in 1808 and backed a prize for explaining it. Sophie Germain won it in 1816 with work that helped found the theory of how solid materials vibrate.
  • Instrument makers still use it. Violin and guitar builders vibrate a top plate dusted with glitter or tea leaves, read its figures, and carve until it rings right. The same physics shapes how cymbals, gongs, bells and drums sound.
  • It's mesmerising. The "cymatics" videos of sand snapping into patterns on a speaker are this experiment.

What CYMA-1 does with it

It turns the experiment into an instrument. Each key makes a virtual plate ring, and the sand on the screen settles along the still lines of whatever is sounding, because the picture and the sound come from the same simulation of the plate's vibration patterns (its modes). Morph the plate from a circle to a hexagon or a star and both the figure and the overtones change together. Press harder and you bow harder, as Chladni did; in SURFACE mode the keyboard itself becomes the plate, and resting a finger on a dark key pulls out a pure harmonic.

CYMA-1's plates are Chladni-like rather than laboratory-exact: they are modelled as stiffened membranes, close enough to draw the classic figures and ring with their overtones.

    How to play

    Every key strikes, bows or rains on a virtual metal plate. The sand on the screen settles into the nodal figure you are hearing, because the picture and the sound are the same modal simulation.

    Keys

    WC♯ ED♯ TF♯ YG♯ UA♯ OC♯ PD♯ AC4 SD DE FF GG HA JB KC5 LD ;E 'F

    A plays C4 and the row continues to the apostrophe, F5, as in Ableton Live; W, E, T, Y, U, O and P are the sharps.

    Mouse, touch and pen

    • Click a key. Higher on the key is softer, lower is harder. In SURFACE mode the very top edge only touches.
    • Drag down on a held key to lean in. A pen leans by its pressure, and so does a Force Touch trackpad in Safari.
    • Shift-click or right-click a key or button to latch it down (Alt-click too); click again to let go.
    • Knobs: drag up and down, or scroll over them. MASTER is the volume.
    • The screen: click it for the big screen.
    • MIDI: play it from a controller (MIDI In), or send its notes and pressure to your DAW (MIDI Out).

    • SELECTShape: drum, circle, hexagon, square, triangle, star, guitar, stadium, bar

    • HOME ENV FX LFO GLOPages: plate, excite, space, motion, global. Tap the page's button again for its layer B, which stays until you tap it again, tap SEL, or change page
    • GLO, heldOn the global page: the boot sound, Team EG's chime, off or on
    • SEQSURFACE: the keybed becomes the plate

    • EDITView: sand, field, mesh
